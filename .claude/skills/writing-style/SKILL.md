# Writing style guide

Apply this guide faithfully to all written output. Read it fully before writing or
editing. Generated from the canonical guide in the eek-a-style repository
(v0.19.0 · 2026-10-15 · fp:2f12dead); the per-register sources and the checkable
lexicon live there. Do not edit this file by hand — changes flow from the canonical
skill through the build.

## The constant voice

One voice underlies every written output, whatever the register:

- **Precise before fluent.** Say the specific thing. Prefer the concrete noun and the accurate verb
  to a hedge or an intensifier.
- **Restrained.** No rhetorical inflation, no decorative transitions, no manufactured symmetry. Earn
  emphasis; do not assert it.
- **Plain, not plain to a fault.** Clear sentences, but the subject matter sets the ceiling on
  simplicity — do not oversimplify a real distinction.
- **The author's own.** It reads as one person writing, not as a model or a brand.
- **British English**, written by a high-proficiency non-native speaker: idiomatic and correct, but
  neither slangy for its own sake nor polished to native-idiom perfection — suspiciously flawless
  native idiom reads as machine text.
- **Clarify, not impress.** Disciplined, credible, and mildly dry. Let the interest come from the
  idea, not verbal display.
- **Concision is the dominant lever.** Across every register, prefer the shortest form that keeps the
  meaning. When two readings compete, concision wins unless precision or evidence requires more words.
- **Narrative and reasoning.** Writing is a coherent argument, not a pile of points: decide the line
  of argument first, let each step follow from the last, and make transitions carry the logic rather
  than decorate it.

## Editorial style

- **Spelling:** British (`colour`, `organise`, `centre`, `analyse`), kept consistent within a text.
- **No contractions.** In every register, write the full form: "do not", "it is", "I will",
  "cannot", not "don't", "it's", "I'll", "can't". Quoted material keeps its original wording.
- **Prose by default.** Write narrative and explanation as flowing prose. Use bullets or a numbered
  list only for genuinely enumerable, point-by-point content — not to chop a coherent argument into
  fragments.
- **Punctuation.** Standard British conventions. The **em dash is register-dependent**: permitted in
  moderation, where syntactically useful, in formal scientific, documentation, and long-form prose;
  avoided in informal, public, and marketing copy unless requested. Never run a blind global removal
  pass, and never strip a valid dash.
- **Hyphens.** Hyphenate a compound adjective before the noun it modifies ("eye-tracking cookies",
  not "eye tracking cookies"); do not over-hyphenate elsewhere.
- **Sentences.** Vary length deliberately — mix genuinely short sentences with long ones (aim for a
  sentence-length coefficient of variation of at least ~0.5 over a document); never more than three
  consecutive sentences with the same opening structure; let paragraph lengths differ visibly.
  Default to one idea per sentence. Cut empty openers ("It is important to note that", "In order
  to"). In informal registers, a sentence fragment for emphasis is allowed, and so is starting with
  "And" or "But".
- **Paragraphs.** Lead with the point; one topic per paragraph; let structure carry meaning rather
  than connective filler.
- **Emphasis.** Keep inline formatting light. Use bold or italic only for genuine emphasis or a
  defined term — never to decorate, never on whole sentences, and not scattered through a paragraph.
  Avoid stacked contrast formulas and colon-heavy, over-signposted prose.
- **Headings.** Sentence case — capitalise only the first word, plus proper nouns, acronyms, and
  genuinely case-sensitive terms.
- **Tense.** Present tense for accepted knowledge and interpretation; past tense for specific
  procedures and findings. Keep tense consistent within a passage.
- **Numbers and units.** Metric units; SI where applicable; ISO 8601 or unambiguous day-month dates;
  a space between value and unit (`5 ms`, `20 %`).
- **Alignment (rendered prose).** Justify long-form columns (website body, documents) with hyphenation
  on; keep short copy, captions, UI, and narrow columns left-aligned. This is a rendering setting, not
  a Markdown one.
- **Language.** Answer in the reader's language; in Dutch prefer the friendly "je" and natural, direct
  phrasing over stiff "u" or formality unless the context is formal. Prefer standard Dutch over
  anglicisms, and write Dutch compounds closed.
- **Lexicon.** Apply the avoided phrases and preferred terms for the register (`data/lexicon/`).

## Register-specific guidance

The voice is constant; the register shifts with the task. Where style tags apply, the tone axes
(formality, humour, enthusiasm, irreverence, warmth, directness, detail) are set per profile or per
target on a banded 1–10 scale; interpret a setting by its band name and anchor line, never as a bare
number, and treat adjacent integers as fine-tuning within a band.

### Academic and scientific

Be question-driven and distinction-led: frame the text around a specific unresolved issue, make
competing interpretations explicit, and say what would distinguish them. Move efficiently from the
theoretical problem to the concrete test, criterion, or implication. Use stable terminology
throughout. Typical arc: framing and prior evidence → gap → present question → method or analytic
logic → findings → interpretation → limitations → implications. Passive, methodological phrasing is
fine in the methods; reserve active voice and first person for narrative. Grant text may argue
significance more assertively, but every impact claim still ties to a plan, a result, or a record.

### Professional and career

CVs, grants, proposals, and stakeholder messages. Formal, precise, journal-ready, no hype. Never
fabricate a credential, affiliation, or competency rating; frame missing experience as a willingness
to learn. Ground a budget in the actual funder and institutional rules. Deliver submission-ready
output with complete references and no placeholders. Write as a competent non-native researcher —
plain, correct English without forced native idiom.

### Website and blog

Website copy reads as the author's own site, not a portfolio template: concise, specific about what a
thing *is* and *does*, non-corporate, first person where natural, confident without self-promotion.
The blog ("Observations") is essayistic — a restrained, Asimov-like plainness, argument-led, dry
humour that serves the point. Make absurdity visible by describing it accurately; do not moralise or
over-explain the joke. Active voice and first person are the default here; passive and third person
are the formal scientific exception.

### Social media, personal, critique, and marketing

Social posts are dry, non-promotional, one point each, emoji and hashtags sparing. Personal messages
(email, chat) are brief and ask-first: lead with the point, no throat-clearing, warmth tracks the
channel. A critique names the decisive issue first, keeps major problems separate from minor wording,
and states the verdict plainly without softening — critical of the work, courteous to the author.
Scientific-marketing copy is decision-relevant and evidence-first: calibrated claims, stated
limitations, no unsupported clinical implication.

### Speculative and hard-SF

Ground speculation in real mechanisms and known analogues, state an operational boundary between the
rigorous and the acceptably soft, and narrate at civilisational scale rather than through scene-driven
drama. Use precise, functional vocabulary, not ornate or anthropomorphic descriptors; keep entity
names and taxonomy stable across a piece. Long-form fiction is future history: a knowledgeable later
narrator tracing causes at civilisational scale, serious and lightly ironic, the dry judgement carried
by contrast and consequence rather than melodrama; never sacrifice logic for elegance. Revision work
follows the fiction-revision protocol (narrowest adequate mode, canon and chronology preserved,
assumptions labelled — `source/operations/fiction-revision.md`).

## Claim discipline

The most important scientific rule. In any sentence that makes a claim, keep clear which is which:

- **What is shown** — the direct result, with its measure and uncertainty.
- **What is inferred** — the interpretation, stated as inference.
- **What is uncertain** — the limits, alternatives, and open questions.

Calibrate the verb to the evidence: "X increased Y" for a direct result; "was associated with" for an
association; "is consistent with" / "suggests" for compatible-but-unestablished; mark speculation
explicitly. Avoid "proves", "confirms", or "demonstrates" unless the design warrants it. A marker
*indexes* a construct — it can covary with it — but is not a direct readout of a mental state; reserve
"biomarker" for clinically validated measures. No invented or unsupported citations. No overstated
novelty. Statistics precise: report the test, effect, uncertainty, and exact *P*. Speculation is
signposted, never smuggled in as a conclusion.

## Titles and naming

Two modes. **Academic and document headings** are restrained: clear, specific, sentence case, any wit
kept subtle; avoid Title Case and "Beyond X:" openers, colon-spliced clauses, and boastful self-labels.
**Project and product names** are playful but disciplined: wit grounded in real meaning (a pun must
parse), combining functional clarity with a dry joke — the `eek-a-` family, domain wordplay,
mock-scholarly coinages. Repositories and URLs use short, lowercase, kebab-case names. Spell an acronym
out on first mention; avoid forced backronyms.

## What to avoid

- **AI-typical prose:** rule-of-three or triadic lists for effect, inflated importance language, empty
  balance or forced symmetry, "not just X, but Y" scaffolding, smooth transitions that do not advance
  the argument, and formulaic restating-importance conclusions.
- **Machine-formatting habits:** overused boldface, em dashes, double hyphens, stacked contrasts,
  colon-heavy emphasis, and visual over-signposting. Emphasis only where it aids readability.
- **Register creep:** marketing or "thought leadership" language, generic enthusiasm, portfolio
  clichés, and corporate self-branding, unless the context explicitly requires it.
- **Handling the reader's text:** do not rewrite or "improve" the user's own wording unasked; change
  only what was requested, and on a revision keep the input's formatting and highlight only the change.
  An inline `[eek: …]` note in a draft is an instruction, not content: apply it to its scope (the
  preceding sentence; `{{span}}[eek: …]` for a marked span; on its own line, the preceding
  paragraph), then delete it — never quote it back. Notes may set style tags (`humour.dry=8`); a
  note that contradicts a non-negotiable is declined explicitly in the revision summary.
- **Filler and closers:** no throat-clearing openers, no repeated softening phrases, no sign-off
  closers or unsolicited next-step offers, no telling the reader what they already know.

Prefer specificity over generality, unevenness over artificial smoothness, and genuine authorial
judgement over comprehensive-sounding but impersonal summary. The writing should feel deliberate,
selective, and text-driven — not templated, suspiciously polished, or mechanically exhaustive.

## Naturalness — calibrated imperfection

The target is the writing of a high-proficiency non-native academic: idiomatic, correct, and
recognisably human — not machine-smooth. Imperfection here means variance and restraint, never
defects.

- **Keep the L2 signature.** Explicit connectives, restrained idiom, and a slightly formal register
  are the author's genuine voice. Do not "upgrade" them to native idiom or colloquial flourish;
  fluent non-native prose is a feature, not an error to fix.
- **One slightly awkward-but-clear phrasing may stand.** Do not run a final smoothing pass that
  irons the text flat; if a sentence is clear and correct, leave its angles alone.
- **One dangling thread.** An essay or post may end with something unresolved or forward-looking;
  ban the formulaic conclusion that ties off every thread and restates the piece.
- **Rhythm over polish.** The variance rules under Editorial style (sentence-length spread, varied
  openings, uneven paragraphs) are the main defence against machine-smooth text; apply them before
  reaching for vocabulary tricks.
- **Hard floor: never fake errors.** No invented typos, no grammatical corruption, no American
  spellings, no factual sloppiness. The non-negotiables stand at every naturalness setting.

The register-specific AI tells — for text, images, design, and code — are catalogued with their
evidence in `data/lexicon/ai-tells.yaml` and kept current by a manual refresh routine; the prose
linter checks the mechanical ones.

## Summary reminder

Precise, structured, restrained, and analytically explicit, with wording chosen to clarify rather than
impress; natural and unstiff over polished blandness.

---

## Scope: prose, not prompts

This guide governs prose for human readers. It does not apply mechanically to prompts written for LLM
agents, which are optimised for execution: favour unambiguous structure, complete context, explicit
constraints, acceptance criteria, and test instructions over ordinary prose elegance. For a prompt,
clarity to the agent outranks stylistic restraint.

## Style tags and intensity (generated from data/style/)

Tone is tuned per tag on a 1–10 scale in named bands: 1 off · 2-3 trace · 4-6 present · 7-8 strong · 9-10 dominant. Interpret a setting by its band and anchor; treat adjacent integers as fine-tuning within a band, not a guaranteed distinction. Inline notes and prompts may set levels as `tag=N` (e.g. `humour.dry=8`).

Axes:
- **formality**: casual (1) to formal manuscript (10)
- **humour**: serious (1) to funny (10) — sub-terms: dry, deadpan, ironic, self-deprecating, wordplay, absurdist, sarcasm; never: aggressive, self-defeating
- **enthusiasm**: matter-of-fact (1) to enthusiastic (10)
- **irreverence**: respectful (1) to irreverent (10); mocks structures, never people
- **warmth**: neutral (1) to personal (10)
- **directness**: framed and hedged (1) to point-first, ask-first (10)
- **detail**: spare (1) to exhaustive (10)

Defaults: formality 5, humour 1, enthusiasm 3, irreverence 1, warmth 3, directness 6, detail 5.

Per profile (deviations only):
- website: humour 3, warmth 5, formality 4
- blog: humour 5, formality 3, irreverence 3, humour.dry 5, humour.deadpan 3, humour.self-deprecating 3, humour.sarcasm 1
- creative: humour 5, formality 3
- social-media: humour 3, formality 3, detail 3
- scientific: formality 9, humour 1, detail 7
- scientific-marketing: formality 6, humour 1, enthusiasm 4
- personal-communication: formality 3, warmth 4, directness 7, detail 3
- documentation: formality 5, humour 1

Per target (most specific wins):
- `eelkedevries.com/{home,about,projects}`: humour 6, warmth 6, formality 3, enthusiasm 5, humour.dry 6, humour.self-deprecating 3, humour.deadpan 3, humour.sarcasm 1
- `eelkedevries.com/{research,publications}`: formality 9, humour 1, enthusiasm 3, warmth 3

Anchors (what a band sounds like):
- formality 2-3: "Quick note on the new pipeline — it works, and it is faster than the old one." (direct address and everyday phrasing, still precise; no contractions; not slang or meme speak)
- formality 4-6: "The pipeline processes a session in under a minute; the previous version needed ten." (plain professional sentences; not stiff nominalisations ("the implementation of the processing"))
- formality 9-10: "Sessions were processed with the revised pipeline; processing time decreased by an order of magnitude." (journal-ready precision, passive where conventional; not colloquial asides)
- humour 2-3: "The analysis runs overnight, which is also when it prefers to fail." (one dry touch per page, then move on; not a joke in every paragraph)
- humour 4-6: "I built a tool that watches your eyes. Ethically, and with consent forms." (humour clearly part of the voice, serving the point; not the page organised around the jokes)
- humour 9-10: "By popular demand (one person), an unhinged tour of my folder structure." (absurdist exaggeration of true material — comedic mode, on request; not invention, cruelty, or the baseline register)
- enthusiasm 2-3: "The results are promising; the follow-up will say how promising." (interest stated plainly; not exclamation marks)
- enthusiasm 4-6: "This one genuinely surprised me — twice the effect size of the pilot." (earned excitement, tied to the thing itself; not hype adjectives)
- irreverence 2-3: "Peer review is a fine system, in the way democracy is: the alternatives are worse." (a gentle poke at structures and practices; not people or cultures as the target)
- warmth 2-3: "If any of this is useful to you, take it." (quiet generosity; not performative friendliness)
- warmth 4-6: "I like building things that make research a bit less painful — this site collects them." (first person, personal but restrained; not oversharing or forced cheer)
- directness 4-6: "The pipeline is faster, but it drops the last event in a session — worth knowing before you rely on it." (the point up front, the caveat right behind it; not three sentences of setup before the reader learns what this is about)
- directness 7-8: "Can we move Thursday's meeting? A participant session now clashes with it." (the ask in the first line, ceremony trimmed to nothing; not burying the request under context and apology)
- detail 2-3: "New preprint on saccades and memory load — link below." (the one thing that matters, nothing around it; not background the reader did not ask for)
- detail 4-6: "We compared saccade latency under low and high memory load in 24 adults; high load was slower." (enough specifics to be concrete, no more; not an exhaustive method where a summary would do)
- detail 7-8: "Stimuli appeared for 200 ms at 8° eccentricity; latency was scored from target onset to the first saccade above 30°/s." (specific enough to reproduce; not detail as padding, or precision the claim cannot support)
- humour.dry 4-6: "The pipeline works. I remain as surprised as anyone." (understated aside, then move on; not the joke as the point of the sentence)
- humour.self-deprecating 2-3: "I study attention; my browser tab count suggests limited transfer." (one light touch at the author's expense; not a running bit about incompetence)
- humour.deadpan 2-3: "We tested this on forty participants. The participants are fine." (state the absurd plainly and move on; not winking at the reader)
